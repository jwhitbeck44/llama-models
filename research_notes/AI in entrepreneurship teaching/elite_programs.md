# AI in Entrepreneurship Teaching at Babson, Stanford, Harvard, MIT, Berkeley, and Comparable Programs

Scope note. Research done October 16, 2026. Every source cited below was opened. Pages that returned 403 or could not be fetched are listed under Gaps, not cited. Each citation gives source type in brackets. "Course page" means a catalog page, syllabus, or program page. Poets&Quants (P&Q) columns written by Steve Blank are labeled as news articles (practitioner column). A full source register is at the end, under the last key question.

## Babson: How does Babson use AI in entrepreneurship teaching? Any course where students build and test MVPs with AI?

### Takeaway
Babson's main documented AI activity is The Generator lab (director Erik Noyes) and its one-day Entrepreneurship and AI Buildathons, where student teams build clickable or working prototypes in six to eight hours. In the first-year FME course, AI is used for brainstorming, personas, simulated research, and supplier lists, not for building a testable product. No Babson source I opened reports students taking AI-built prototypes to real customers or any measured outcome.

### Cited Findings
- The inaugural Generator-Microsoft Entrepreneurship and AI Buildathon drew more than 200 students. Projects had to be built at the event with no prework. Tracks were Entrepreneurial AI for Well-Being, AI-Powered Entrepreneurship, and AI Safety and Ethics. Named tools were Codium, Cursor AI, and BoodleBox.ai. The winner (Factuality, a fact-checking Chrome extension) received $2,500. Source: [Baskin, "How Student Teams Used AI to Tackle Real-World Problems at One-Day Buildathon," Babson Thought & Action, Dec 12, 2024; news article (institutional)](https://entrepreneurship.babson.edu/generator-ai-buildathon/)
- That article names Erik Noyes as director of The Generator and associate professor of entrepreneurship. Noyes said: "The event is proof of concept that there's a huge appetite with students to mash up entrepreneurship and AI." It does not say prototypes were tested with real users. It says Factuality planned a Chrome Web Store launch. Source: [Baskin, Babson Thought & Action, Dec 12, 2024; news article](https://entrepreneurship.babson.edu/generator-ai-buildathon/)
- In an April 2026 interview, Noyes said the April 11, 2026 Buildathon (the fourth, one per semester) drew about 500 students from 35 universities and about 60 teams. He said: "We're getting clickable prototypes, we're getting working physical prototypes, within the course of six to eight hours." He said he had not yet analyzed the portfolio of teams. Source: [Yeranossian (interviewer), "The Entrepreneurship Renaissance: How AI Is Expanding What Students and Small Teams Can Actually Build" (Erik Noyes interview), PrometAI "Between Campus and Code," Apr 28, 2026; newsletter/blog (vendor-hosted interview)](https://prometai.app/expert-insights/erik-noyes-interview-ai-entrepreneurship)
- In the same interview, the only real-person contact described is a session pairing students with small business owners to give "multiple angles on some prototypes Microsoft was considering." Noyes also reported (self-reported, unverified) that over 50% of about 200 Babson faculty were peer-trained in AI, that AI is built into the first-year venture program of about 800 students, and that he is coordinating a crowdsourced book, *AI Entrepreneurship Education*, with 25 faculty contributors. Source: [PrometAI interview, Apr 28, 2026; newsletter/blog](https://prometai.app/expert-insights/erik-noyes-interview-ai-entrepreneurship)
- FME (Foundations of Management and Entrepreneurship) is Babson's first-year experiential course. Teams of four develop and launch a venture over two semesters with up to $3,000 in seed funding. Students used Microsoft Copilot for product ideas, customer personas, and simulated market research. Some had AI answer surveys "in the voice of" target consumers. AI produced product designs, then students built paper prototypes. Co-directors Ruth Gilleran and Lucy Turner are quoted. Gilleran: "Your team of four is now five." The article reports no sales, customer, or learning outcomes. Source: [Chabot, "AI Joins the Team: How FME Students Are Learning to Use Generative AI," Babson Thought & Action, Mar 3, 2025; news article (institutional)](https://entrepreneurship.babson.edu/fme-students-use-ai)

### Inferences
- Babson is a leader in volume and visibility of AI prototyping events, but the documented build-and-test activity is extracurricular (buildathons), not a credit course with a real-customer test requirement.
- FME is the closest Babson analog to the requester's course (first-year, many students, real ventures). Its documented AI use is upstream (ideation, personas, simulated research), not AI-built test versions shown to real people.
- FME's use of AI to answer surveys "in the voice of" consumers is an example of simulated stakeholders, which Blank's Stanford experience (below) flags as a risk if it substitutes for real people.

### Gaps
- No Babson syllabus or course page found that requires students to build an AI MVP and test it with real customers.
- No outcome data from buildathons or FME on prototype quality, user tests, or sales.
- Noyes's crowdsourced book *AI Entrepreneurship Education* was mentioned in the interview only. I did not find a published version.

## Stanford: Which courses and instructors use AI in entrepreneurship, what do students build, and do they test it?

### Takeaway
Stanford has the single best-documented case: Steve Blank's Lean LaunchPad. In 2025 and 2026 all eight teams used AI to build working MVPs in hours while still interviewing over 900 real stakeholders per year. Blank's 2026 after-action review concluded that cheap AI MVPs produced "evidence theater," and he redesigned the course for fall 2026 around tested "Snippets," design partners, and human interviews that grade synthetic ones. Stanford GSB has many AI courses. One (Kostas Bimpikis) reports non-technical students building working MVPs in one to two weeks, but with no real-customer testing reported.

### Cited Findings
- Lean LaunchPad at Stanford, spring 2025 (15th year). Teaching team: Steve Blank, Steve Weinstein, Lee Redden, Jennifer Carolan. Eight teams "spoke to 935 potential customers, beneficiaries and regulators." Blank wrote: "This was the first year where all teams used AI to help create their business model canvas, build working MVPs in hours, generate customer questions, analyze and summarizing interviews." The post does not name the AI tools in its text and does not say MVPs were shown to customers. No course number is given. Source: [Blank, "Lean LaunchPad at Stanford – 2025," steveblank.com, June 24, 2025; newsletter/blog](https://steveblank.com/2025/06/24/lean-launchpad-at-stanford-2025/)
- Pre-AI comparison point: the 2024 class had eight teams that "spoke to 919 potential customers, beneficiaries and regulators." Source: [Blank, "Steve Blank: Stanford's Lean LaunchPad 2024: 8 Teams In, 8 Companies Out," Poets&Quants, June 25, 2024; news article (practitioner column)](https://poetsandquants.com/2024/06/25/steve-blank-stanfords-lean-launchpad-2024-8-teams-in-8-companies-out/)
- Spring 2026 (16th year): eight teams interviewed 978 potential customers, beneficiaries, and regulators. All teams used AI for canvases, MVPs "in hours," customer questions, and interview analysis. Tools used by everyone were Claude and Granola. Others included ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude Code, Replit, Lovable, Listen Labs, Viewpoints AI, NotebookLM, and Perplexity. Blank wrote that "an MVP is no longer evidence of critical thinking and hypothesis testing" and that teams "pivoted late as they assumed that a polished product meant product/market fit." Source: [Blank, "Lean Launch Pad 2026 @ Stanford – Lessons Learned Presentations," steveblank.com, June 16, 2026; newsletter/blog](https://steveblank.com/2026/06/16/lean-launch-pad-2026-stanford-lessons-learned-presentations/)
- P&Q reported that every 2026 team arrived with a finished, working product at the start of class, which Blank had not seen in 16 years. Blank: "An MVP is now equivalent to a PowerPoint slide." In a synthetic-interview experiment, students compared AI-generated interviews with real ones. The article reports half the synthetic interviews were hallucinating but gives no sample size or method. Source: [Ethier, "The Man Who Disrupted Entrepreneurship Education Says AI Just Disrupted Him," Poets&Quants, July 5, 2026; news article](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/07/05/the-man-who-disrupted-entrepreneurship-education-says-ai-just-disrupted-him/)
- Blank's own after-action account: "Students used AI instead of customers for insights and validation." "AI had created evidence theater." "Teams collected compliments instead of disconfirming evidence." "Creating products rapidly at almost no cost had allowed teams to make bad ideas go faster." He gives a pre-AI baseline of 10 to 15 stakeholders per week, 100+ per quarter, and 3 to 4 pivots per team. No 2026 sign-up or revenue data is reported. Conclusions rest on teaching-team observation. Source: [Blank, "The Year AI Came For Us: Teaching Entrepreneurship Will Never Be The Same," Poets&Quants, Sept 21, 2026; news article (practitioner column)](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/09/21/the-year-ai-came-for-us-teaching-entrepreneurship-will-never-be-the-same/)
- Blank now labels AI-built day-one products "Initial Untested Products (IUPs)." He wrote that "MVPs were also no longer reliable evidence of customer discovery, critical thinking, hypothesis testing." He also wrote: "We want students to use AI to the max, just like in the real world." Source: [Blank, "AI Killed The MVP – Long Live The IUP," Poets&Quants, Sept 24, 2026; news article (practitioner column)](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/09/24/ai-killed-the-mvp-long-live-the-iup/)
- Redesigned Lean LaunchPad. Evidence units are "Snippets" (a small part of the IUP used to test one value-proposition hypothesis), a "Minimal Tested Canvas," and design partners. Requirements include at least 10 recorded human interviews per cycle, "Stanford students do not count as interviews. 50% need to be in person," and each interview logged as hypothesis, experiment, expected result, actual result, path forward. "Students will be graded on the quality of their outreach, not on the success of proving their snippet correct." Synthetic interviews are run weekly but "the live interviews grade the synthetic ones, never the reverse." AI-generated work must be labeled. Source: [Blank, "Steve Blank: Lean LaunchPad – The Next Generation," Poets&Quants, Oct 1, 2026; news article (practitioner column)](https://poetsandquants.com/?p=286534)
- Stanford GSB: P&Q reports "some three dozen courses" highlighted or integrated AI in 2025-26 and that "Startup Garage now kicks off with a 60-minute AI-powered hackathon." Named AI-and-startup courses include HRMGT 384, "The Future of AI in Work: A Lab for Startups" (Huggy Rao, Michael Ross, Prasad Setty) and OIT 351, "AI and Data Science: Strategy, Management and Entrepreneurship" (Kuang Xu). The article does not describe what students build or whether they test with customers. Source: [Byrne, "The New MBA Arms Race: How Stanford Is Winning On AI," Poets&Quants, Apr 22, 2026; news article](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/04/22/stanford-gsb-and-ai/)
- Kostas Bimpikis (Stanford GSB) taught "Digital Platforms in the Age of AI" for the first time in spring 2026. Students used Colab with Gemini, Lovable, Claude, and Cursor. Bimpikis: "Students with zero technical background built working MVPs" and "it's almost as easy to build a small working prototype as it is to build a PowerPoint." Prototypes, pitch videos, and presentations took roughly one to two weeks. No real-customer testing or outcome numbers are reported. The article links an "oit277" project site but does not state the course number. Source: [Olenick, "Michael Olenick: The Deck Is Dead," Poets&Quants, July 14, 2026; news article (interview column)](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/07/14/michael-olenick-the-deck-is-dead/)

### Inferences
- Stanford Lean LaunchPad is the closest existing analog to the requester's idea at an elite school: AI-built working products plus heavy real-person contact. It is a team-based, application-only, graduate-heavy course, not an intro undergraduate solo course.
- Interview totals were 919 (2024), 935 (2025), and 978 (2026). AI did not visibly reduce the count of real conversations in aggregate. Blank's concern is the quality of evidence, not the count. These numbers are not a designed comparison.
- Blank's redesign offers concrete guardrails that fit the requester's measures: count only real humans, require in-person share, log hypothesis and result per test, grade outreach quality, and label AI-made work.
- Blank's account is a strong, public warning that "real customer evidence" needs a definition. Compliments and polished demos can look like evidence. A rubric that distinguishes commitment (sign-ups, pre-orders, repeat use) from praise would address this.

### Gaps
- Stanford course numbers for Lean LaunchPad are not stated in any source I opened. I did not invent one.
- I found no Stanford d.school or STVP course page describing AI-built prototypes tested with real users. Not searched exhaustively.
- A Stanford Continuing Studies course "TECH 36, Vibe Coding: Using AI for Programming" appeared in search results only. I did not open it, and it is not an entrepreneurship course.
- No quantitative outcome data (sign-ups, revenue, grades) from any Stanford course. The synthetic-interview "half hallucinating" figure lacks a sample size.

## Harvard: Do HBS or Harvard students build products with AI and test them with customers?

### Takeaway
Jeff Bussgang says AI is a focal point of his HBS course Launching Tech Ventures, and his book argues AI cuts the cost of MVPs and customer simulation. I found no source describing HBS students building AI products and testing them with real customers in a course. HBS's documented AI product is HBS Foundry, an eight-week $699 online bootcamp with AI faculty avatars for pitch and sales-call practice (simulated stakeholders), with no outcome data beyond enrollment.

### Cited Findings
- Bussgang: AI "has become a focal point in my Harvard Business School (HBS) MBA course, Launching Tech Ventures." The excerpt advises founders to "Use AI coding tools to create an MVP in 1/10th the time" and to simulate customer conversations and surveys. It contains no student examples or numbers. Source: [Bussgang, "The AI-Savvy Founder: The Next Frontier of Startup Experimentation," HBS Working Knowledge, Mar 10, 2025, excerpt from *The Experimentation Machine: Finding Product-Market Fit in the Age of AI* (Damn Gravity Media, 2025); book (excerpt)](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/ai-savvy-founder-the-next-frontier-of-startup-experimentation)
- HBS Foundry is an eight-week virtual bootcamp costing $699. Participants practice pitches, sales calls, and boardroom scenarios with AI video avatars of faculty built with HeyGen, including one of Bussgang. Tom Eisenmann is a faculty co-chair. Katharina Rings is project director. At least 760 founders had taken it and more than 100 universities tested it. A Georgia Tech instructor (Harold Solomon) added it to a 12-week course. No completion or venture outcome data is reported. The article does not say participants test products with real customers. Source: [Shibu, "Harvard Is Offering a New $699 Bootcamp for Entrepreneurs, But There's a Catch," Entrepreneur, Aug 25, 2026; news article](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/harvard-is-offering-a-new-699-bootcamp-for-entrepreneurs-but-theres-a-catch)

### Inferences
- Harvard's public AI-in-entrepreneurship activity leans toward AI as coach and simulated stakeholder (avatars, simulated customers), not AI-built test products taken to real people.
- Bussgang's book is a citable practitioner source for the premise that AI lowers MVP cost, but it is not evidence about student outcomes.

### Gaps
- No HBS syllabus or news story found showing LTV students vibe-code an MVP or run landing-page tests.
- The Harvard Innovation Labs event page "AI Coding: How to Build your Product Faster" returned 403. Not cited.
- No Harvard College or SEAS undergraduate course found on this topic. Not searched exhaustively.

## MIT: What do the Trust Center's AI tools do, and do students test with real people?

### Takeaway
MIT's AI work centers on JetPack, a generative AI tool inside the Trust Center's Orbit platform that walks students through Bill Aulet's 24 Disciplined Entrepreneurship steps and drafts segments, pricing, and plans. It is a coaching and drafting tool, not a product builder. MIT staff stress that students must still talk to real customers. The only measured data is an MIT master's thesis on 15.390 showing higher course ratings, more hours, and more step iterations after Orbit, which are student perceptions and usage logs, not real-world evidence.

### Cited Findings
- JetPack is "a generative artificial intelligence tool trained on Aulet's 24-step Disciplined Entrepreneurship framework." In a beta example it suggested market segments, beachhead market, business model, pricing, and product plan within seconds, covering seven of 24 steps. It runs in Orbit, launched for students in 2019. Aulet: "it's like having five or 10 or 12 MIT undergraduates who instantaneously run out and do all the research." No usage or outcome numbers are given. Source: [Somers, "New AI JetPack accelerates the entrepreneurial process," MIT News, Sept 19, 2024; news article (institutional)](https://news.mit.edu/2024/new-ai-jetpack-accelerates-entrepreneurial-process-0919)
- In the delta v accelerator, students used AI for coding, presentations, research, and brainstorming but "still ultimately relied on talking to their customers." Entrepreneur in Residence Ben Soltoff: "AI alone can't tell you who your customer is, what they want, and how you can better serve their needs." Macauley Kenney: "You need to verify everything when you are using AI to build a business." No outcome metrics. Source: [Winn, "How are MIT entrepreneurs using AI?", MIT News, Sept 22, 2025, opened via MIT OGE mirror; news article (institutional)](https://oge.mit.edu/?p=105265)
- William Dale's MIT thesis (advisor Bill Aulet) studied Orbit in 15.390 across Spring 2024, Fall 2024, and Spring 2025 using usage logs and aggregated course evaluations. Findings: pre-Orbit Fall overall ratings averaged 5.5 (1 to 7 scale), rising to 5.9 in Fall 2024 with Orbit v2. A composite of three learning items reached 6.1. "Assignments contributed to my learning" rose from a pre-Orbit average of 5.6 to 6.1. Hours outside class rose from 5.3 (Fall 2023) to 5.5 (Spring 2024, v1) to 6.2 (Fall 2024, v2). Monthly active users peaked near 45, 51, and 74 across the three semesters. The thesis says aggregated evaluations do "not permit a direct correlation between individual student tool usage" and outcomes. It reports no customer-discovery or venture outcomes. The text has some internal inconsistency on which semester reached 5.9. Source: [Dale, "Emergent Dynamics in AI-Enhanced Entrepreneurship Education: A System of Systems Study of the Orbit Tool" (title page in PDF reads "AI and the Human Element: Exploring the Collaboration Between Entrepreneurs and Artificial Intelligence in Decision-Making and Venture Outcomes"), SM thesis, MIT System Design and Management, May 2025; thesis (not peer-reviewed), DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/162539) and [PDF copy on d-eship.com](https://www.d-eship.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/dale-wdale-sm-sdm-2025-thesis.pdf)
- A vendor case study says MIT's Disciplined Entrepreneurship course deployed 24 AI assistants, one per step, built on StackAI, with "Over 250 students actively engaged" and "students saved more than 500 hours." No baseline or method is given. These are vendor claims. Source: [StackAI, "How MIT Deployed 24 AI Assistants in 2 Weeks, Saving 500+ Student Hours," stackai.com, undated; vendor marketing page (closest type: newsletter/blog)](https://www.stackai.com/customers/how-mit-transformed-an-entrepreneurship-course-with-ai)

### Inferences
- MIT is the strongest case of AI as a structured coach through an entrepreneurship framework. It is not a case of AI lowering the cost of building a test version for real people.
- The thesis is the only source in this set with a before-and-after comparison across cohorts. It is a useful model for the requester's design (prior cohort as comparison), but its measures are satisfaction and usage, not pitch quality or customer evidence.

### Gaps
- No MIT source found on students using AI build tools (Lovable, Bolt, etc.) for MVPs tested with real users within 15.390 or delta v.
- No data on whether JetPack use changed the number or quality of real customer interviews.

## Berkeley: Any AI-based MVP building or testing in courses?

### Takeaway
Berkeley has two strong examples. Haas's new MBA course AI Entrepreneurship (Omri Even-Tov and Alex Zekoff, spring 2026) ran as a 13-week accelerator where 27 teams built prototypes with Claude Code, Lovable, and other tools and aimed to "test in the market." SCET's ENGIN 183 (open to all students) explicitly targets "actual customer adoption and revenue generation," and its fall 2026 version aims at "putting it in front of paying customers." Neither source reports customer counts, sign-ups, or revenue.

### Cited Findings
- Haas "AI Entrepreneurship," spring 2026. Co-creators Omri Even-Tov (associate professor, accounting) and Alex Zekoff (MBA 17). GSI Devansh Shah. More than 300 applications, 27 teams, 57 MBA students, 13 weeks, more than 130 mentors. Tools provided through credits included Claude Code, Lovable, AWS (including SageMaker), and Manus. Zekoff: teams can "build prototypes and test in the market and get a signal before you go raise." Demo Day was April 23, 2026. Twenty teams committed to continue and six were accepted into accelerators such as Berkeley SkyDeck. One team (Populous) builds simulated populations for customer discovery. No real-customer test counts, sign-ups, or revenue are reported. Even-Tov: "I was still surprised by how much progress they made in just 13 weeks." Source: [Girard, "Skip the Seed Round? New MBA course is turning students into founders at the speed of AI," Berkeley Haas News, May 6, 2026; news article (institutional)](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/new-ai-entrepreneurship-course-is-a-hands-on-startup-accelerator-for-mba-founders/)
- A week earlier, Haas News called the course "AI and Entrepreneurship," with 57 full-time and evening and weekend MBA students. It also described the StEP incubator (director Kelly Ochoa, 18 teams) where teams used AI to build demos faster, with an emphasis on "validating real markets, deeply understanding customers." It mentions Lean LaunchPad at Haas as a class where students build faster with AI, with no detail. Source: [Girard, "At the epicenter: How MBA students are leveraging Berkeley's AI boom," Berkeley Haas News, Apr 29, 2026; news article (institutional)](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/at-the-epicenter-how-mba-students-are-leveraging-berkeleys-ai-boom/)
- ENGIN 183-001, "AI-Powered Startups: Build, Validate, and Scale at Warp Speed," Fall 2025, instructor Khalil Fuller, 2 units, "open to all students" with instructor consent. Teams develop and market functional technology products, aiming for "actual customer adoption and revenue generation." The course ends with a Demo Day presenting products and traction to peers, industry, and investors. No metrics are defined on the page. Source: [UC Berkeley Class Schedule, ENGIN 183 001, Fall 2025; course page](https://classes.berkeley.edu/content/2025-fall-engin-183-001-sem-001)
- The SCET page for the Fall 2026 offering (ENGIN 183/283-001) is titled "Next Generation AI-assisted Product Development." It covers idea generation, building with little or no code, content creation, and marketing from zero. "Each week builds toward a single goal: shipping something real and putting it in front of paying customers." No instructor or outcome data is listed on that page. Source: [Berkeley SCET, "Next Generation AI-assisted Product Development," Fall 2026; course page](https://scet.berkeley.edu/students/courses/ai-powered-startups/)

### Inferences
- Berkeley ENGIN 183 is the closest documented match to the requester's design in structure: open to non-business students, AI-assisted building, and a goal of real customers and traction. It differs in being a team course and an elective, with no published evidence data.
- The Haas course shows elite MBA programs now treat AI-built prototypes as normal, but its public evidence is Demo Day and accelerator admissions, not customer evidence.
- The Populous team illustrates the trend of simulated customers replacing real ones, the same pattern Blank warns about.

### Gaps
- No Haas course number found for AI Entrepreneurship. Name differs between the two Haas articles.
- No Demo Day traction data for ENGIN 183.
- No SkyDeck or Jacobs Institute course evidence found on AI-built MVPs tested with users. Not searched exhaustively.
- I did not confirm whether the Berkeley Method of Entrepreneurship (SCET) has formal AI components.

## Other notable programs: documented courses where students build with AI and test with real users

### Takeaway
Many schools now have students build working apps or prototypes with AI (Penn EMBA with Mollick, UMD Smith with Wendy Moe, Imperial Enterprise Lab, Rice, HKU). Only a few course descriptions also require putting the product before real users (Imperial's bootcamp plan to reach 20 potential users, HKU's learning outcome on acting on customer feedback, Rice's ENTR 365). None I found reports measured real-world evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Penn (Ethan Mollick). In an experimental class, mostly executive MBA students with little coding experience had four days to build a startup. They used Claude Code and Google Antigravity to build, and ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini for ideas, research, pitching, and financial models. Mollick: "Most of the prototypes were not just sample screens but actually had a core feature working." He judged them "an order of magnitude further along the path to a real startup" than pre-AI semester-long student work. He said they were not yet working startups, with a couple of exceptions. No real-customer testing or measurement is described. Source: [Mollick, "Management as AI superpower," One Useful Thing, Jan 27, 2026; newsletter/blog](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/management-as-ai-superpower)
- University of Maryland Smith. Wendy Moe's fall 2025 MBA course Marketing in the Age of AI: "Every student in my class used vibe coding to build an app for their final project." Students used Replit and other tools. Moe said several apps were close to launch-ready. No real-user testing or class size is reported. Source: [UMD Smith, "Smith Among Early Movers Bringing Vibe Coding into the MBA Experience," Feb 9, 2026; news article (institutional)](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/news/smith-among-early-movers-bringing-vibe-coding-mba-experience)
- Imperial College London. The Imperial Enterprise Lab Vibecoding Bootcamp is a three-day online sprint for "Students from any background," with no coding needed. Day 1 includes a plan to reach 20 potential users. Day 2 builds a prototype in Lovable and tests whether people can understand and use it. Day 3 shares it through a channel and ends with a 2-minute pitch. No outcome data. The year is not stated in the page text. Source: [Imperial Enterprise Lab, "Vibecoding Bootcamp: A 3-Day Startup Sprint from Idea to Prototype," event page; course page](https://www.imperialenterpriselab.com/events/vibecoding-bootcamp-2/)
- Rice University. ENTR 222, AI and Tech Product Development (3 credits), uses AI "as a research engine, synthesis tool, and rapid-prototyping partner." ENTR 365, Design Build Ship (3 credits), has students build prototypes rapidly and test with users. Catalog text only. Source: [Rice University General Announcements, Entrepreneurship (ENTR) courses; course page](https://ga.rice.edu/programs-study/courses/entr/)
- University of Hong Kong. Course 4671 "AI Entrepreneurship," 2026-27, instructor Ankit Suri. Decoded learning outcomes include building and deploying "a functional AI-enabled MVP" and acquiring and acting on customer, buyer, and market feedback. The PDF only partly decoded, so assessments could not be read. Source: [HKU Business School, IIMT4671 course outline 2026-27; course page/syllabus](https://ug.hkubs.hku.hk/f/course/255890/26_27-IIMT4671.pdf)
- Non-AI precedent for landing-page tests. Teaching Entrepreneurship's "60 Minute MVP" has teams build a one-page landing page in one class period that states the problem, shows a video, and asks for "currency" such as pre-orders. It names Celery for pre-orders. It names no AI tools and reports no class data. Source: ["Exercise: 60 Minute MVP," Teaching Entrepreneurship, Feb 18, 2026; newsletter/blog (teaching resource)](https://teachingentrepreneurship.org/60-minute-mvp/)

### Inferences
- "Students build a working app with AI" is now widespread across MBA and some undergraduate settings. "Students put it in front of real people and count the result" appears mostly as a stated goal in course descriptions, not as reported results.
- Imperial's bootcamp design (reach 20 users, test usability, share through a channel, 2-minute pitch) is structurally close to the requester's last-four-weeks plan, but extracurricular.

### Gaps
- Columbia Business School: both pages I tried (CBS Insights article on new AI courses and catalog page B8160 "Vibe Coding") returned 403, and web.archive.org could not be fetched. Search results suggested a fall 2026 Vibe Coding course with a public demo day, but I could not confirm it.
- NYU Stern (Foundations of AI Agents), Yale SOM ("Coding with Kyle" student group), Tuck (vibe coding workshop), and Korea University appeared in search results only. Not opened, not cited.
- I found nothing on Michigan Ross, UT Austin McCombs, Oxford Saïd, or INSEAD courses where students build with AI and test with real users. Searches were limited.
- Wharton beyond Mollick was not found.
- A Maven listing claimed an instructor taught 60 Berkeley students to build with AI. It is a vendor claim seen only in a search snippet. Not opened, not cited.

## Outcome data: Is there evidence of effects, or only course descriptions and news stories?

### Takeaway
Almost all evidence is course descriptions, institutional news, and instructor anecdotes. The only systematic data are (a) MIT thesis course evaluations and usage logs for Orbit in 15.390, and (b) Blank's yearly totals of real stakeholder interviews in Stanford Lean LaunchPad. No source reports sign-ups, landing-page conversions, user-test counts, share of pitches citing customer evidence, or blind-scored comparisons against a pre-AI cohort.

### Cited Findings

### Takeaway

## Overall judgment: Is "students use AI to build a real test version and take it to real people, measured by real-world evidence" common, partly done, or new?

### Takeaway
Partly done. AI-built prototypes in entrepreneurship courses are now common at elite schools. Taking them to real people is done in some courses (Stanford Lean LaunchPad, Berkeley ENGIN 183 by stated goal, Imperial bootcamp, HKU by stated outcome). Measuring and publishing the real-world evidence those tests produce, and comparing against a pre-AI cohort, was not found anywhere. The specific combination the requester proposes (intro undergraduate, many non-business majors, solo ventures, AI-built test version, about five real-user tests plus sign-ups, counted evidence, blind rescoring against a prior cohort) appears new in the public record I could find.

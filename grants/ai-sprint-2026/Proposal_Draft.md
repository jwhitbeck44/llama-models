## Proposal

### 1. Proposed use of AI to improve student performance

ENTP 3000 is an in-person course of about 42 students in seven permanent Peer Advisory Boards of six, many of them not business majors. This project targets Course Learning Outcome 2: "explain the challenges and issues that entrepreneurs face when starting a venture," which the syllabus lists as resource constraints, market uncertainty, team conflict, leadership without authority, and the gap between ideation and execution.

In Phase 3 (Modules 12 to 15) each student builds a solo venture: ethnography, a low-fidelity prototype, a business model, and the Final Pitch. The prototype is built in class and critiqued by the student's Board. Whether anyone outside the class sees it is up to the student **[CONFIRM against the Design Thinking Part II brief]**. So the gap between ideation and execution, one of the five challenges in Outcome 2, is left optional in the student's own venture.

The project changes Module 13 from a task completed in the room to one launched in class and done in the field, as Modules 2, 3, 4 and 6 already are, and it changes the Final Pitch brief. In the Session 13 Studio block, each student writes on paper with their Peer Advisory Board the one claim the test will check (who will want this and what they will do), who those people are, and what result would count as failure. No AI is used in this step. In the Machine Shop, the course's live projected AI segment, I build one student's test version with the build tool, then run one prompt that lists the reasons a stranger would not sign up. The week's Founder Task follows: each student builds a test version with the build tool outside class, puts it in front of at least five people outside the class, and records what happened. The artifact is the live link, a log of who saw it and what they did, and the student's reading of the result. At Session 14 the Board works out what the result means for the business model. The Final Pitch brief then asks for the test and its result, whatever it was.

The task is scored on the Founder Task rubric, extended from the weekly tasks to this one: Evidence of the Act, Specificity, Framework Contact, Honest Reckoning. Under that rubric a test that nobody signed up for, described precisely, can earn full marks on all four criteria. The instructor of the closest precedent reported that AI-built products led teams to collect "compliments instead of disconfirming evidence" (Blank, 2026a). The rubric scores the act and the honesty of the report, not the result.

AI builds the artifact and nothing else. The student states the claim, recruits the people, and reads the result. The build tool is meant to let a student with no technical training produce a presentable test version within the week, and the license means that does not depend on who can pay. A student who has watched five strangers decline can explain the gap between ideation and execution from their own case, and the Final Exam then asks for the same explanation about another founder without AI.

### 2. Context within AI-for-pedagogy literature and campus practice

Building a test version with AI is now common in graduate courses at Stanford and Wharton (Blank, 2026b; Mollick, 2026). Requiring real people appears in Blank's redesign for 2026 to 2027, not yet run, which requires recorded human interviews and does not count fellow students as interviews (Blank, 2026c), and in a 2026 lesson plan where each student builds an app with an AI tool and watches one friend use it (Wilcox and Mammano, 2026). None of these reports how many people each student reached, how many pitches cite evidence from real people, or a blind comparison with an earlier cohort. The instructor of the closest course concluded, after a term of building with AI and using AI for customer insights, that "the impact on learning far outweighed the benefits AI provided" (Blank, 2026a). This project adapts his fixes (Blank, 2026c): one claim, a small artifact, outreach graded rather than success, only people outside the class, AI-built work labeled.

In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school students, unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice scores and lowered the later closed-book exam by 17%, while a version that gave hints rather than answers showed no significant loss (Bastani et al., 2025). In a field experiment with 640 Kenyan business owners, an AI business mentor hurt below-median owners and may have helped above-median owners (Otis et al., 2026). Neither study tested build tools. In this project AI builds, the student states the claim and reads the result, and one measure has no AI present.

On campus, UCCS licenses ChatGPT Edu for students who complete its AI literacy course. Among the twelve Spring 2026 Sprint Grant awards, AI tutors and chatbots were the most common design, the one business award is a sales simulation (Ploumitsakos, CU Boulder), and the four UCCS awards put AI between human steps, in role-play, in clinical decision checks, and in a chatbot for teacher candidates (McGuire, Haisley, Vandenberg, Smith with DeBay) (CU Office of Academic Affairs, 2026). None puts an AI-built artifact in front of people outside the classroom and counts what happens. From searches of named programs and journals, not a census, what appears new is the setting (an introductory undergraduate course with many non-business majors and solo ventures) and the measurement (counted evidence from real people and blind rescoring against a prior cohort), not the build step, building with AI, or requiring real people. No study found compares students with and without technical or business backgrounds on building with these tools, so the counts are reported by major and background.

### 3. Assessing learner outcomes

Outcome 2 is assessed through the DIY Mentor Project, the Final Pitch, and the Framework Application section of the Final Exam, and all three stay. The Final Pitch brief changes in one way: the solution section must report the test and its result. The new task is scored on the existing Founder Task rubric, and because that rubric rewards an honestly described failure, its scores are not an outcome measure. Fall 2026 is the comparison cohort: same instructor, same Module 12, same Final Pitch rubric, no build tool. Both cohorts can produce every measure.

1. Number of people outside the class who saw or used what the student built, from the student's log, checked against records. Fall 2026 students answer the same question with the December 2026 Final Pitch.
2. Share of Final Pitches citing evidence from people outside the class: a response, a sign-up, a refusal, or a user getting stuck. Board members' comments and AI output do not count.
3. Blind rescoring of about 84 Final Pitches from both cohorts, names and dates stripped, order randomized, scored in one window by three trained student raters from neither cohort, on the existing Final Pitch rubric plus one evidence criterion (0: no evidence from real people; 1: evidence described; 2: evidence described and something changed because of it). I report the two-way random-effects intraclass correlation with its 95% confidence interval and raters' guesses of each pitch's cohort as a check on blinding.
4. The Framework Application section of the Final Exam, where AI is not permitted, scored on the same exam rubric in both years **[CONFIRM the Fall 2026 exam rubric will be reused]**.
5. Indirect: the anonymous AI Check-In survey in week 1 and a repeat in week 15 on paid versus free tool use, with major and first-generation status.

Limits. This compares two cohorts, not randomized arms, so time and section differences are mixed with the change, and if the Fall 2026 brief did not ask for a test with real people the comparison estimates the new task and the build tool together **[CONFIRM]**. About 42 students per cohort can detect a difference of about 0.6 standard deviations, so smaller effects will appear as estimates with wide intervals, and this is a pilot reporting effect sizes with confidence intervals.

### 4. Replicability across courses or disciplines

The design fits any course in which students make something for people outside the room: a marketing course testing an offer, a nursing course testing patient materials. The package is a one-page task brief, the Studio worksheet (the claim, the people, the failure condition), the log template, the evidence criterion with rater instructions, and the tool-access plan with costs. Another instructor needs a build tool license and a rubric that rewards an honest account of a failed test.

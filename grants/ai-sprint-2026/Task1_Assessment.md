# Task 1: the research report against the plan

Prepared 2026-10-16 from the deep research report (AI_in_entrepreneurship_teaching.md), the Fall 2026 syllabus draft, the Founder Task rubric, FOR-NEXT-YEAR.md, the midterm feedback summary, and the grant call and form.

## Verdict

Keep the plan. The main project is partly done elsewhere and new in two respects: the setting and the measurement. The report changes the design in small ways, changes the measures in larger ways, and narrows the novelty claim. None of these is a substantial change to the plan, so I rewrote the proposal without stopping. The changes are listed below so you can reverse any of them.

## Is the main project common, partly done, or new?

Partly done. Each part exists somewhere. No found course combines the parts in an introductory undergraduate course and reports results.

- Building a cheap test version and putting it in front of real people predates AI. Steve Blank's 2011 Lean LaunchPad used low-fidelity sites and fake sign-up forms. The "60 Minute MVP" lesson plan (TeachingEntrepreneurship.org) uses a landing page taking pre-orders.
- Building the test version with AI is now common in graduate courses: Stanford Lean LaunchPad 2025 and 2026 (Claude Code, Replit, v0, Lovable), Wharton (Mollick's four-day class, January 2026), Berkeley Haas (spring 2026, 57 MBAs), Stanford GSB (Bimpikis), Maryland Smith (fall 2025 MBA), Babson buildathons.
- Requiring real people appears in Blank's 2026 to 2027 redesign (10 recorded human interviews before class one, classmates do not count), Berkeley ENGIN 183 ("paying customers"), Imperial's three-day bootcamp (20 potential users), and a June 2026 lesson plan by Wilcox and Mammano where each student builds an app in Lovable and watches one friend use it.
- None of these reports counts of real people reached per student, the share of pitches citing customer evidence, or a blind comparison with a non-AI cohort. Their evidence is instructor observation, course pages and school news. Yu et al. (2025) found published work in this area mostly conceptual or small and exploratory.

Closest examples, in order: Blank's Lean LaunchPad (graduate teams, weekly interview targets, 978 stakeholders in 2026); the Wilcox and Mammano lesson plan (one student, one app, one friend); Imperial's bootcamp; Mollick's four-day class.

Ethan Mollick: his closest case is the January 2026 four-day class, mostly executive MBAs with "few had ever coded," building prototypes with Claude Code and Google Antigravity. He reports no customer contacts, sign-ups or user tests, and credits the results to the students' professional experience. His five teaching papers (2022 to 2024) are working papers with prompts and no learning data. His usable positions for the proposal: "always generate your own ideas before turning to AI" (July 2025); do not use AI "when the effort is the point" (December 2024); his AI user-test report was "pretty optimistic" until he asked for a critical one (January 2026). He has not endorsed simulated customers as a substitute for real ones.

## What the evidence says should change

### Design

1. Blank's warning from the closest course. With AI-built products, teams "collected compliments instead of disconfirming evidence"; "the impact on learning far outweighed the benefits AI provided." His fixes: test only a small piece that checks one hypothesis; grade the quality of outreach, not success; classmates do not count; recorded human interviews; label AI work. The plan already matches two of these through the Founder Task rubric (a precisely described failure earns full marks) and the phase 3 disclosure line. I added the other three to the task: the student writes the one claim the test checks and what result counts as failure before anything is built; people outside the class only; a log that the count is checked against (Blank also reports students "padding the number of interviews").

2. Division of labor between student and AI. In the causal studies, learning fell when AI substituted for the student's reasoning (Bastani et al. 2025, PNAS; Lehmann et al. working paper) and did not fall when AI was a complement. In the only field trial on AI and entrepreneurial decisions, AI advice hurt below-median entrepreneurs because of which advice they chose to act on (Otis et al. 2026, Management Science). Non-business majors start with less business knowledge, so this risk applies here. The design now states that AI builds the artifact only; the student states the claim, recruits the people, and reads the result. This is also the course's existing rule: production restricted, rehearsal and critique permitted.

3. The optional AI critique. Every practitioner source accepts an AI objection pass before real people and none accepts it as validation. No study tests whether one AI critique improves the later real-world test, and AI feedback runs optimistic. I moved it from an individual step to the Session 13 Machine Shop: you run one objection prompt on one student's page on the projector. It stays in the existing format, costs nothing, adds no individual AI assignment, and is not a measured component.

4. Data from real people. A public vulnerability record covers Lovable-generated sites through April 2025 (database tables readable or writable). Default: sign-up forms run in Microsoft Forms under the UCCS Microsoft 365 license, collecting a name and email only, deleted at term end; the AI-built page carries the offer and links to the form.

### Measures

5. Overalignment. If the Fall 2026 Design Thinking Part II brief does not require putting the prototype in front of anyone outside the class, the count of real people reached and the share of pitches citing real evidence give the Fall 2027 cohort an advantage by assignment, and the comparison estimates the whole package (new assignment plus AI tools), not the AI tools. I could not check, because the Phase 3 task documents were not attached. The proposal says both cohorts could produce the counts and names the comparison as a package comparison. Action for you now: add one line to the December 2026 Final Pitch submission asking how many people outside the class saw or used the prototype and who they were. This is ordinary course work, costs nothing, and gives the baseline count. Keep the Fall 2026 pitch videos.

6. The outcome is not measured without AI. Outcome 2 is "explain the challenges and issues." The counts and the pitch are AI-assisted outputs. Several studies show AI-assisted output and later unaided performance diverging (Bastani; Barcaui 2025; Contractor and Reyes 2026). The Final Exam framework application section is already unaided (AI not permitted), exists in both cohorts, and targets Outcome 2. It is now a named measure. Keep the exam questions or at least the exam rubric the same across the two years.

7. Blind rescoring protocol. The old draft had two raters scoring Fall 2026 pitches in spring 2027 and Fall 2027 pitches in December 2027. Raters then know which batch is which, so the blinding fails. Now: pool both cohorts, strip names and dates, randomize order, score in one window (December 2027 to January 2028), three raters who were in neither cohort (Koo and Li 2016 recommend at least three and a two-way ICC with its confidence interval), calibrate on practice pitches from neither cohort, and have raters record a guess of each pitch's cohort as a blinding check. AI-built prototypes look different, so the guess check matters.

8. Power. With about 42 students per cohort, the comparison can detect a difference of about 0.6 SD (0.44 to 0.52 SD with covariates). Reported AI effects in classrooms and firms run 0.14 to 0.4 SD. The proposal says so and frames the project as a pilot reporting effect sizes with confidence intervals. I did not add a within-semester randomized arm. With 21 per arm it detects only 0.89 SD, it splits the Board structure, and it would not change what a reviewer can conclude. If you want an AI-only estimate, the cheapest route is a second comparison cohort (two prior cohorts against two AI cohorts detects about 0.43 SD), which does not fit one grant cycle.

9. Rubric scores. As you noted, the Founder Task rubric rewards an honestly described failure, so Founder Task scores are not an outcome. The counts, the evidence criterion on the pitch, and the exam are.

### Novelty claim

10. Narrowed to what the report supports. Not new: the build step, building with AI, requiring real people, an AI objection pass. New: an introductory course open to every major with solo ventures and no cost to students; counted real-world evidence; blind cross-cohort rescoring; an unaided exam measure. Among CU Spring 2026 awards, none puts an AI-built artifact in front of people outside the classroom and counts what happens.

11. Inclusion stated as a hypothesis. The evidence supports only that AI makes a first testable version cheaper and faster. Non-programmers succeeded less often than trained students in coding studies, and gaps by gender and first-generation status persist with equal access. The proposal reports the counts by major and background rather than claiming that the tools close the gap.

12. Access. UCCS licenses ChatGPT Edu for all students (ai.uccs.edu/chatgpt; AI Literacy at UCCS course required since August 11, 2026). No build tool is licensed. Free tiers (Lovable: 5 build credits a day) allow one build and little iteration, so the license line stays. Lovable prices by shared credit pool, not seats, with per-member limits, which fits a class.

## Is there a better option in the report?

No. The report's own recommendation is the package comparison plus one within-semester randomized design; I explain in item 8 why I left the randomized arm out. Blank's rule to test only a small piece that checks one hypothesis is a refinement the task now uses. Nothing in the report points to a different project with a clearer outcome measure.
